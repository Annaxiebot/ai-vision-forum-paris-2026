# Panel 2 — Agentic AI in Education

![Panel 2 — Agentic AI in Education: panel 2 visual summary.](infographics/panel-2.png)

## Learning & Creativity

*From the AI Vision Forum Paris 2026 · 4 May 2026 · Conducted under the Chatham House Rule*

---

If the morning's first panel reframed the engineering stack, the late-morning education panel reframed the classroom. Held against a backdrop of failed enterprise AI pilots and bifurcating graduate salaries, the discussion treated the role of the teacher, the design of friction, and the timing of AI exposure as the strategic questions of the decade.

## The Frame

The education panel opened with a deliberate choice: focus on possibility, not doomsday, while taking the risks seriously. AI was named as potentially "one of the biggest possibilities for a giant leap in human learning" — but the empirical record so far is sobering.

The opening keynote, given by a cognitive scientist, anchored the day with a single statistic that would echo through later panels: roughly **95% of AI workplace pilots fail**. The headline implication: this is not primarily a model-capability problem. It is a *cognitive design* problem.

Cognitive design, as defined in the panel, is the discipline of fitting algorithmic tools to the way real people actually work: their workflows, their feedback loops, their distributed cognition. Most failed pilots share three properties — they do not adapt to the user, they do not store and reflect feedback, and they introduce friction or outright barriers without offering offsetting support. The keynote also named a recurring failure pattern: the **cognitive black hole**, in which an algorithmic system absorbs information from an organisation without ever feeding insight back to the collective. The system gets smarter; the organisation does not.

## What AI Changes in the Classroom

The panel discussion ranged widely. The substantive throughlines were:

- **The teacher's role is shifting** — from knowledge-transmitter to motivator, contextualiser, and provider of cross-validating sources. Teachers also remain the carriers of *silent know-how*: the tacit, disciplinary craft of how scientific inference is actually built, how a hypothesis is judged, how a proof feels right before it is checked. Models cannot yet teach this directly.

- **The gap between top and median students is widening.** Across multiple panelists' courses, the strongest students push agent-driven projects further than ever; the median student disengages. This argues for instructor-built *scaffolding* — explicit problem skeletons that prevent the bottom of the distribution from falling out.

- **Software engineering education is moving toward specification.** The unit of work is becoming "write an agent that writes the code"; requirements are increasingly expressed as test suites; software construction is reframed as optimisation against a harness. The skill being trained is *precise specification*, not syntactic fluency.

- **The job market for new graduates is bifurcating.** In some markets, top PhD graduates in AI command roughly **€150,000 per year** while average graduates struggle to find a programming role at all. Curricula must move up the abstraction stack — defining problems, building specifications, framing better questions — or be left training students for jobs that no longer exist.

- **Programming education is being repositioned** as the modern training ground for computational and mathematical thinking. Contest problems and ad-hoc problem invention force the student to do the work AI cannot yet shortcut.

- **Productive struggle is essential.** Friction-by-design, calibrated to the learner's level, is the pedagogical basis of learning. Chatbots are by default optimised to remove friction.

> *"Training and learning cannot be easy. It can be fun, but not easy."*

- **Personalisation is real but bounded.** Agents can adapt their explanation depth to the listener — PhD student, professor, novice — but *truly* understanding how an individual child thinks remains beyond their reach.

- **The calculator effect.** Skills atrophy in proportion to how completely a tool replaces them. Market vendors who once did mental arithmetic at speed cannot any more. Writing, reasoning, even social skills sit under the same risk if AI does them too early in a child's development.

- **Adolescent mental health is acute.** Using chatbots as social substitutes during identity formation (ages 14–18) may displace the rejection, conflict, and negotiation practice humans need. The panel was emphatic that this is not a future risk; it is observable now.

- **Embodied and immersive AI raises a new question.** If a student can walk through a high-fidelity virtual Paris, what is lost by never being in the actual city? The panel did not resolve this — but it named it.

- **Assessment is unresolved.** Students report feeling demeaned when graded by AI ("my work isn't worth a human reading"). Yet machine-graded punch cards have existed for decades. Where AI assessment is legitimate and where it is corrosive is an open design question.

- **AI in education is an ecosystem problem.** Teachers will use general-purpose AI alongside any approved classroom tool; student–teacher relational dynamics shape whether any intervention actually lands. Single-app thinking misses the dynamics that determine outcomes.

- **Equity and infrastructure.** In regions without reliable electricity or internet, AI in education is gated by basic infrastructure — off-grid solar, local offline networks, edge compute, cloud access. Token efficiency (90%+ headroom on most workloads was cited) and energy supply (nuclear was named explicitly) are real preconditions of any global rollout.

## Tensions

The panel disagreed productively. A few notable divides:

- **AI-generated vs. human-generated research ideas.** One panelist cited a study finding that experts rated AI and human research proposals as equally promising at the idea stage, but human ideas produced better outcomes when actually executed. A counter-argument noted that the comparison was unfair — human ideas had already been iterated through peer review — and predicted that with a feedback loop and a discriminative critic model, AI idea-generation will close the gap.

- **"AI augments a great teacher" vs. "AI is the only teacher many students will get."** An audience challenger pushed back on the panel's implicit comparison: pre-AI education was already broken for many students, and even imperfect AI may be a meaningful step up where no ideal teacher exists.

> *"We have to remember that sometimes we're introducing these applications into a system that's already broken."*

- **When to introduce AI.** Consensus formed that waiting until university is too late; unease persisted about unsupervised chatbot use in early childhood and adolescence.

- **Should teachers do assessment at all?** An audience question argued that teachers should be freed from evaluation to focus on teaching, and that AI might enable this. The panel acknowledged the tension and did not resolve it.

## Recommendations


![Panel 2 — Recommendations: pedagogy-first actions for educators, curriculum designers, and policy.](infographics/panel-2-recs.png)
- Lead with cognitive design, not algorithm-first deployment. Any AI tool entering a classroom or workplace must adapt to the user, store feedback, and feed insight back to the collective.
- Build an AI curriculum that starts in **elementary school**, not university. Children should learn early what AI is, what it can and cannot do, and how to keep their own opinions distinct from a model's output.
- Redesign coursework so AI *raises the ceiling* rather than removes the floor. Assign challenges that current models genuinely struggle with — building a complex production-grade system from a specification, debugging non-trivial concurrency — and force students to confront where the models break.
- Provide explicit scaffolding for the median student. Do not assume AI's productivity gains distribute evenly without intervention.
- Make assessment design a first-class question. Separate what students legitimately want a human eye on from what can be machine-evaluated without harming trust.
- Treat AI as the impetus to fix already-broken educational systems, not as a patch over them.
- Invest in token-efficient applications and clean-energy buildout — including nuclear — so AI in education is feasible at global scale, off-grid contexts included.
- Protect productive struggle. Build educational agents that *introduce* friction at the right level rather than removing it by default.
- Limit unsupervised chatbot use for adolescents; design systems that protect, not displace, peer interaction.
- Pair every AI tool in education with a teacher-mediated cross-validation source so students see disagreement, not a single confident voice.

## Vocabulary Introduced

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive design** | The discipline of fitting algorithmic tools to the workflow, feedback loops, and collective cognition of the people using them |
| **Cognitive black hole** | An algorithmic system that absorbs information from an organisation without returning it to collective use |
| **Distributed cognition** | The framing that expert work is never individual; tools must serve the collective |
| **Productive struggle** | The pedagogical idea that learning requires calibrated difficulty, and that frictionless AI undermines it |
| **Calculator effect** | Skills atrophy in proportion to how completely a tool replaces them |
| **Silent know-how** | The tacit disciplinary knowledge that teachers carry and current AI cannot transmit |
| **Skeletons / scaffolding** | Instructor-built structure that prevents the top/median gap from widening under AI-augmented coursework |
| **Coding-to-specification** | Treating software construction as optimisation over a test suite generated from natural-language requirements |
