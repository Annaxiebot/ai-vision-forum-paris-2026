# Panel 4 — Open Token & Digital Public Goods

![Panel 4 — Open Token & DPGs: panel 4 visual summary.](infographics/panel-4.png)

## Foundations & Sustainability

*From the AI Vision Forum Paris 2026 · 4 May 2026 · Conducted under the Chatham House Rule*

---

The day's closing panel returned the conversation to infrastructure. If the morning was about how agents change software engineering, and the afternoon was about how to govern them, the late-afternoon session asked the harder long-run question: who pays for the substrate, and how do we keep it open?

The panel ended with the four-point **Paris Initiative**, committed to public publication on GitHub and a follow-up milestone at GOSIM Shenzhen.

## The Frame

The framing was historical: open source built the modern digital world — Linux at the bottom, Python in the middle, Rust securing the next-generation systems — and the LAMP era's success depended on a quiet consensus that the substrate was a *shared resource*. The AI era introduces a new strategic resource — **compute, expressed as tokens** — that sits alongside code as foundational infrastructure. The question is whether that substrate will be open in the same way, or whether the next decade's dependency graph will be entirely closed.

A "Linux for AI" has not yet emerged. The current AI stack is a five-layer system in which open-source projects and closed-source giants coexist at every layer; the opportunity is to build community-driven infrastructure at the layers where it is still possible. The moderator framed three rounds for the panel — *why now*, *governance and sustainability*, and *global collaboration* — anchored to the question of whether tokens can be treated as a new digital public good.

Regional strengths were named as complementary rather than competitive:

- **Europe** contributes innovation depth and governance maturity.
- **China** contributes scale, engineering volume, and rapid product cycles.
- **The Global South** contributes both need and largely-untapped talent — large African AI conferences host thousands of young contributors who remain globally invisible.

## What Sustainable Open AI Looks Like

The substantive throughlines of the discussion:

- **Three pillars of resourcing.** Cash, in-kind compute (tokens, hardware time), and human capacity must be treated as *co-equal*. No single pillar substitutes for another. The persistent industry temptation to throw in-kind compute at maintainer burnout and call it support was explicitly named as inadequate.

- **The contribution avalanche.** AI-assisted contributions to core open-source projects have surged on the order of **+110% in six months**, while maintainer headcount and budgets are flat.

> *"We were held together with dental floss and gum. That is now actually stretched to its limit."*

- **Quality and security strain.** New contributors are bypassing community norms, submitting unreviewed AI-written code, and relying on automated translation that can erase prior human work. Trust within maintainer communities is being eroded, not because the tools are necessarily bad, but because community norms have not yet adapted.

- **Maintainer burnout, not money, is the most acute bottleneck.** Many maintainers are at the edge of quitting. AI has multiplied the volume of incoming contributions without proportionally easing the cognitive load of review.

> *"AI does not equal more free time and less stress for maintainers."*

- **AI as a public utility.** A recurring analogy: tokens should be governed like an electric grid. GPUs run whether they are used or not; off-peak pricing, pooled access, and tiered allocation can democratise use without driving cost to zero — because zero price destroys the discipline that makes any utility sustainable.

- **Democratising access through Open Token.** Affordable tokens for students, researchers, hackathon participants, and open-source maintainers let people experiment bravely across a rapidly-shifting framework landscape (LangChain → MCP → agents → emerging coordination protocols) without being priced out of learning.

- **Fragmentation is a feature, not a bug — but it is expensive to navigate.** Open Token reduces the cost of trying multiple frameworks and models, which in turn improves the rate at which the ecosystem converges on what actually works.

- **Cross-continent contribution flows are uneven.** Chinese labs are contributing open-weight models across size tiers, from edge models to trillion-parameter releases. European communities concentrate on governance, licensing, and digital-rights compliance. The Global South contributes labour and ideas largely invisibly.

- **AI as digital public good is not automatic.** The UN's DPG Alliance has qualified only **three AI systems** out of 241 total Digital Public Goods. The DPG standard goes beyond an open license: it requires platform independence, documentation, privacy protections, and a "do no harm by design" property — none of which most AI systems currently meet.

- **Compute governance is emerging as a policy lever.** Monitoring who trains what at what scale, steering subsidies toward public-interest training, and embedding cryptographic verification at the hardware level are early instruments of a new regulatory field — one that begins to bind the conversation back to Panel 3's verification themes.

- **Local and edge models complement Open Token.** Small models running on-device offer self-control, privacy, and effectively "free" usage where the user already paid for hardware and electricity. Cloud tokens remain higher-quality for frontier tasks. The right architecture is hybrid, not exclusive.

- **"Free" is never free.** Free tokens are typically a marketing funnel that monetises data or lock-in.

> *"No meal is free. Only one meal is free. If later you use free token, you will pay in another way."*

The goal is *affordable*, *governed* token access — not zero-price tokens that incentivise over-consumption and external environmental cost.

## Tensions

- **Should tokens be cheap or expensive?** One view: affordable Open Token is the path to democratisation. Another: tokens being too cheap accelerates environmental damage; an ethical price floor should be considered.

- **Free vs. open.** "Free tokens" (a marketing tactic) and "open tokens" (governed, transparent, provenance-verifiable) are not interchangeable terms.

- **Charity vs. strategy in corporate contribution.** One panelist rejected the framing of "charity" entirely — all contribution is enlightened self-interest in a long arc. A foundation perspective pushed back: donor strings and PR demands often make this feel unfair to maintainers on the receiving end.

- **Fairness of allocation.** Foundation-run cloud-compute programmes already provoke accusations of favouritism. Consensus formed around the need for neutral, government- or foundation-mediated public pools rather than single-company gatekeeping.

- **Local vs. cloud.** Tension between the prediction that "everything will be local in five years" and the present reality that frontier quality, speed, and multi-modal capability still favour large cloud providers.

- **Competition vs. collaboration among frontier labs.** One view held that competition is healthy and will deepen markets. Another worried about race-to-the-bottom dynamics — 10% margins on Chinese cloud were cited — eroding the commercial base that funds open contribution.

## The Paris Initiative

The panel closed with a four-point initiative agreed in the room and committed to public publication on GitHub, with a follow-up milestone at the next GOSIM gathering in Shenzhen:

1. **The AI era requires new infrastructure beyond code** — tokens, compute, governance.
2. **AI compute is critical infrastructure** and must be governed accordingly.
3. **Access to that infrastructure must be designed for everyone** — students, researchers, maintainers, the Global South — not as charity but as ecosystem hygiene.
4. **The work is multi-stakeholder and global by construction**; no single jurisdiction, foundation, or company can produce a legitimate result alone.

## Recommendations


![Panel 4 — Recommendations: foundation, industry, and policy actions for Open Token and DPGs.](infographics/panel-4-recs.png)
- **Foundations and companies.** Resource open AI infrastructure on three pillars — cash, in-kind compute, human capacity — and stop substituting one for another.
- **Public bodies.** Build neutrally-governed token pools — analogous to public compute consortia like the ICANN-style arrangement at ETH Zurich — for students, researchers, and Global South contributors. Multi-year grants, not one-month vouchers.
- **Standards work.** Develop a "green ethical Open Token" standard with provenance, environmental-impact disclosure, and DPGA-style nine-indicator qualification for AI systems.
- **Compute governance.** Adopt hardware-level cryptographic verification, monitored allocation, and subsidy routing toward public-interest training.
- **Industry.** Companies running coding-agent ecosystems should sponsor maintainers directly with token grants and triage capacity. Allow employees to spend leftover subscription credits on triaging issues in open projects they depend on.
- **Open-source projects.** Raise contribution-acceptance standards (reproducible test cases, manual QA, peer review) to absorb the AI-generated PR avalanche without quality collapse.
- **Governance design.** Adopt bottom-up grassroots models that surface Global South and African talent rather than relying solely on top-down structures.
- **Allocation transparency.** Reward open-source contribution with token access (PR-, issue-, hackathon-based eligibility), with allocation transparent and never used to bias model improvement back to the donor.
- **Measure outcomes.** Build leaderboards and feedback loops so token grants are measured by *output*, not just consumption.

## Vocabulary Introduced

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Open Token** | A governed, transparent, affordable token-access regime treated as shared infrastructure for the AI era, analogous to open source for code |
| **AI Factory / Token Factory** | Frontier labs reframed as production facilities for tokens rather than just models |
| **Digital Public Good (DPG)** | UN/DPGA designation requiring open license plus eight further indicators (ownership, platform independence, documentation, privacy, do-no-harm-by-design, etc.) |
| **AI as DPG** | Extension of DPG to AI systems, requiring openness of weights, training process, and data, not just code |
| **Compute governance** | Emerging policy field treating compute allocation as a regulatory and steering instrument |
| **Token avalanche** | The surge in AI-generated contributions to open-source projects (cited at +110% in six months) |
| **Three pillars of resourcing** | Cash, in-kind compute, human contribution |
| **Edge tokens vs. cloud tokens** | The distinction between on-device inference (effectively free at point of use) and cloud-served frontier model tokens |
| **Paris Initiative** | The panel's four-point declaration on AI infrastructure as a digital public good |

## Closing — The Day's Synthesis

The closing synthesis from the day's host reframed the question that opened the forum — *as AI becomes more autonomous, who is responsible?* — as one no single panel was meant to answer. What emerged instead was the beginning of a shared language: **synergy as a contract rather than a feeling, learning as more than efficiency, trust as engineered rather than performed, and public goods as a design choice rather than an afterthought.** This language was named as Paris's contribution to the broader AI conversation, with explicit invitation to carry it into GOSIM Paris and onward to the next gathering in Shenzhen.
